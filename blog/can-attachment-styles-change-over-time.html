Can Attachment Styles Change Over Time?

Have you ever noticed how you respond differently across your relationships? Perhaps you worry a lot about whether someone cares about you, feel uncomfortable when someone gets too close, or struggle to trust others even when they have given you reasons to.

These patterns may be related to your attachment style.

Attachment theory explores how our early relationships with caregivers can influence the way we understand closeness, trust, emotional safety, and connection. In turn, these experiences shape our attachment patterns.

Many people have taken an attachment style test, or have identified their style through their own research online, and often end up labelling themselves with that style. This raises a common question: do attachment styles change, or do they stay with us forever? Before answering, let's look at the four types of attachment styles.

What Are the Four Types of Attachment Styles?

Both childhood and adult attachment are commonly described using four types:

  • Secure attachment: Feeling relatively comfortable with both emotional closeness and independence, and being able to seek support while respecting personal boundaries.
  • Anxious attachment: Experiencing concerns about rejection or abandonment, which may lead to seeking reassurance or feeling uncertain in relationships.
  • Avoidant attachment: Feeling uncomfortable with emotional dependence or vulnerability, and tending to prioritise independence or distance when relationships become emotionally demanding.
  • Fearful-avoidant (disorganised) attachment: Desiring closeness while also fearing being hurt or rejected, which may lead to conflicting responses to intimacy.

If you are not familiar with the research behind these categories, it comes from Mary Ainsworth's “Strange Situation” experiment (Ainsworth et al., 1978), which built on John Bowlby's attachment theory. In it, a caregiver and child were placed in a room full of toys, and researchers observed how the child behaved towards the caregiver, including after brief separations. Children's behaviour differed depending on their attachment style.

The categories reflect two key dynamics: proximity-seeking and exploration. Children naturally seek proximity to their caregivers in order to feel safe, and that sense of safety allows them to explore. However, depending on how their caregivers respond, children learn different ways of relating to them.

Early Experiences Matter, but They Aren't the Whole Story

Research suggests that adult attachment patterns show some degree of stability. In other words, people may continue to experience similar thoughts, feelings, and behaviours in their relationships over time.

However, stability does not mean that change is impossible. Although early caregiving experiences can influence how we approach relationships, they do not determine how we will relate to others throughout our lives. Fraley and Roisman (2019) highlight that the relationship between early experiences and adult attachment is more complex than we might assume.

Experiences in adulthood can also play a role. For example, someone who grew up feeling that their emotional needs were often overlooked may become particularly sensitive to signs of rejection. They might seek reassurance or worry when someone becomes less responsive. However, through experiences of being understood, respected, and supported, they may gradually develop different expectations of relationships.

This does not mean that one positive relationship will automatically change someone's attachment style. Rather, it shows that our experiences can influence how we understand closeness, trust, and emotional security over time.

So, What Influences Attachment Patterns in Adulthood Then?

Our attachment patterns can be shaped by more than our childhood experiences. Relationships, significant life events, and our understanding of ourselves may all affect how we experience emotional closeness and security.

1. Romantic and close relationships

The way others respond to our emotional needs and vulnerabilities can influence how safe we feel in a relationship.

For example, someone who tends to feel anxious may become more comfortable expressing their needs when they experience consistent communication and emotional responsiveness from a partner. Similarly, someone who finds emotional closeness difficult may gradually feel more comfortable opening up when their boundaries are respected and they are not pressured to share more than they are ready to.

2. Significant life experiences

Experiences such as separation, grief, betrayal, relationship breakdowns, or major life transitions can influence how we experience trust and emotional safety. For some people, difficult experiences may reinforce existing concerns about rejection or abandonment. For others, supportive relationships and meaningful experiences may contribute to a greater sense of security.

Fraley, Gillath, and Deboeck (2021) explored how life events were associated with changes in adult attachment. Their findings suggest that some events were linked to immediate changes, although enduring changes were less common on average. This highlights that attachment can change, but the process is not necessarily straightforward or permanent.

3. Self-awareness and reflection

Becoming more aware of our thoughts, emotions, and relationship patterns can help us understand why we respond in certain ways. For instance, you might notice that you tend to withdraw when someone tries to get emotionally close, or that you become particularly worried when a partner takes longer than usual to reply to a message.

Rather than judging these reactions, exploring them with curiosity can help you recognise the experiences, fears, or expectations influencing your responses. Self-awareness alone does not necessarily change an attachment pattern, but it can be a meaningful starting point for understanding yourself and considering different ways of relating.

How Counselling Can Help

If you notice recurring patterns in your relationships that leave you feeling distressed, disconnected, or uncertain, counselling can provide a supportive space to explore these experiences. You can reflect on how your past and present experiences influence the way you relate to others, better understand your emotional needs, and explore different ways of responding in your relationships.

Understanding your attachment patterns is not about finding something wrong with you. It is an opportunity to become more aware of yourself, your relationships, and the possibilities for change.
